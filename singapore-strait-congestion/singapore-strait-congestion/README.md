It extends Part 2's baseline-edge finding to the Singapore series and adds a lead-lag result.** The first fitting window (2020-01-01 to 2020-03-30) is artifactual for the Singapore port-call baseline too, with the opposite sign to Malacca (mean z +4.45 for Singapore against -3.86 for Malacca): Singapore's model under-forecasts that window where Malacca's over-forecasts it. > **Update added 2026-10-15, after Part 2. The Part 1 text below is unchanged, but two qualifications apply.** (1) The match-rate result stands (it is also 64%, 78 of 122 clusters, if the first 90 out-of-sample days are excluded), but it shows that flagged anomalies sit near documented events, not that the events produce a distinctive anomaly path: the Part 2 event study, which lines the z-score up on each of the six event start dates, finds an average path that is not distinguishable from randomly placed dates (post-minus-pre signed z -0.42, placebo p = 0.12; |z| +0.15, p = 0.15; without COVID, p = 0.15 and 0.28). (2) The sentence below about the four largest anomalies (z up to 7.3) all landing in the COVID-19 window reads stronger than the evidence supports: all four clusters are in January to March 2020, the baseline's first fitting window, where a model fitted on a single year over-forecasts traffic (on the largest days it forecast about 225-240 vessels against 133-140 actual, while the actual year-on-year fall was 11-34%). The fall was real, but those z-scores overstate its size and should not be read as a measure of magnitude. See the Part 2 bullets below.

- Pulled 7.7 years of daily vessel-transit data (2019-01-01 to present) for the Malacca Strait and the Port of Singapore directly from the IMF/Oxford PortWatch public ArcGIS API, alongside PortWatch's own documented disruption-events calendar, using a `requests`-based pagination script rather than a static download.
- **Model selection** (`python model_selection.py`, also printed at the top of `analysis.py`): compared three candidate targets (Malacca daily vessel count, Singapore daily port calls, Singapore daily trade value) under the identical rolling out-of-sample method: log-transform, trend + day-of-week + month baseline refit quarterly on the trailing 2 years, scored only on the following quarter, |z| > 2.5, clustered, matched to the event calendar within 14 days. Results:

  | Target | Mean training-window R^2 | Out-of-sample R^2 | Clusters | Matched within 14 days | Match rate if clusters were placed at random |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Malacca vessel count | 0.230 | 0.383 | 129 | 83 (64%) | 56% |
  | Singapore port calls | 0.347 | -0.283 | 83 | 60 (72%) | 56% |
  | Singapore trade value | 0.073 | -0.004 | 65 | 52 (80%) | 55% |

  The choice of Malacca does not rest on a clean R^2 win. In-sample, port calls fit the training windows better (0.347 vs 0.230). Out of sample, which is what an anomaly detector actually depends on, Malacca is the only candidate whose forecast beats a flat average (0.383); the port-calls baseline does worse than the mean on the days it scores (-0.283), and trade value is about zero. Malacca was kept because of that out-of-sample fit and because it is a direct count of vessels through one chokepoint, with the most flagged days and largest anomalies. Matching to documented disruptions does not separate the candidates either: all three beat the random-placement rate, and Malacca's lift (64% vs 56%) is the smallest, so disruption matching is supporting evidence, not a reason to prefer Malacca. An earlier version of this note cited R^2 = 0.45 for Malacca; that was the static trend model rejected below, and the old trade-value figure (0.03) was never produced by the committed code.
- Built the baseline with a rolling 2-year re-fit (numpy OLS, refit quarterly) instead of one static trend extrapolated forward for years; an initial static-trend version flagged anomalies almost exclusively in the final year of data and none in 2024, a sign of trend-extrapolation drift rather than genuine disruption, so it was rejected in favor of the rolling design before drawing any conclusions from it.
- Validated flagged anomalies against a ground-truth calendar of documented global shipping disruptions (COVID-19, Red Sea Tensions, Panama Canal drought restrictions, the Francis Scott Key Bridge collapse, the US ports strike, the 2026 Strait of Hormuz tensions): 83 of 129 flagged anomaly clusters fall within 14 days of a documented event (64%, against about 56% expected if the same clusters sat at random dates, since the calendar covers much of 2020-2026), and the four largest anomalies in the entire 2019-2026 series (z-scores up to 7.3) all land inside the COVID-19 window, correctly identifying it as the single largest genuine disruption rather than treating it as noise.
- Flagged, rather than hid, weaker matches: two 2026 clusters coincide with the Strait of Hormuz tensions despite Hormuz having no direct shipping-lane overlap with Malacca, so they're reported as a plausible but mechanistically unconfirmed link, not stated as a confirmed cause.
- Ran a robustness/sensitivity check (`sensitivity_analysis.py`) on the headline 64%-vs-56% result across a 5×5 grid of anomaly thresholds (|z| > 1.5 to 3.5) and event-matching windows (±3 to ±30 days): the real match rate beats the random-placement chance rate in all 25 grid cells, from +2pp at the loosest threshold up to +28pp at the strictest, confirming the real-vs-chance gap isn't an artifact of the specific (2.5, 14-day) choice reported above. See `data/chart_sensitivity_heatmap.png`.
- Built an event study around the six documented disruptions (`event_study.py`), the reverse of the match-rate check above: instead of asking whether anomalies fall near events, it lines up the Malacca out-of-sample z-score on each event's start date (45 days either side) and averages across events, for signed z and for |z|, with two uncertainty bands, a 95% interval across the events and a placebo envelope from 2,000 draws of randomly placed event dates. The average path is not distinguishable from random dates. Comparing the 30 days from the event start against the 30 days before it, signed z moves by -0.42 (95% interval across events -1.16 to +0.32; the placebo range is -0.46 to +0.63, two-sided p = 0.12) and |z| by +0.15 (placebo p = 0.15); without COVID, -0.41 (p = 0.15) and +0.08 (p = 0.28). Three of the six events have at least one |z| > 2.5 within 14 days of their start (two of five without COVID), but so does a randomly placed date 54% of the time. The two largest shifts are Hormuz (mean z +0.09 before, -1.37 after) and the Panama Canal restrictions (+0.47 to -0.58); among the five clean events the other three barely move (+0.13 to +0.20), and with five events none of this is statistically reliable. See `data/chart_event_study.png`.
- Found a baseline edge artifact while building the event study, and checked what it does to the headline: the very first model (refit quarterly, 2-year window) is fitted on only one year of data, and it over-forecasts January 2020 at 226 vessels a day against an actual 177, which is 3.6% above January 2019, not below it. That is why the COVID event's z-score is already around -3 before its start date: an artifact, not a traffic drop. It is also why the all-six-events average path dips below the placebo envelope on 17 of the 46 post-event days (but 1 of 45 before) while the five events from November 2023 on sit inside it, outside on 2 of 91 days, about chance. The edge affects only 7 of the 129 flagged clusters (5 of them matched), and excluding that first 90-day window leaves the match rate at 64% (78 of 122), so the headline result above is unchanged. The event study therefore reports all six events and the five without COVID side by side.
- **Tested whether Malacca or Singapore anomalies show up first (Part 3: `lead_lag.py`). Neither does: the two move together on the same day, with no detectable lead in either direction.**
  - *Does the repo's data cover this?* Mostly. Both files already carry per-vessel-type columns (Malacca `n_container`, `n_dry_bulk`, `n_general_cargo`, `n_roro`, `n_tanker`, `n_total`; Singapore the matching `portcalls_*`), so the vessel-type breakdown needs no new data, and it is run for the total, container and tanker pairs (dry bulk is left out: Singapore logs about 2 dry-bulk calls a day against Malacca's 51 transits). What the repo does not hold, and PortWatch does not publish, is a Singapore *Strait* series: the chokepoint layer lists 28 chokepoints and none is Singapore. The Singapore side here is therefore the Port of Singapore (arrivals), not a strait, so this compares transits through Malacca with port calls at Singapore, which is also the natural physical pair. Neither file records direction of travel. The smallest fix for a strait-to-strait comparison does not exist in PortWatch; the realistic routes are MPA's Maritime Data Hub (the first extension below) or the Sunda and Lombok straits (`chokepoint19`, `chokepoint15`, fetchable with the existing `fetch_chokepoint(portid)`), which are the alternative routes and a different question. Neither was done here.
  - *Method.* The anomaly series are the existing out-of-sample z-scores (`baseline.rolling_oos_fit`, unchanged) for each pair; k > 0 means Malacca leads Singapore. Cross-correlation for lags -14 to +14 with a null band from 2,000 circular shifts of the Singapore series (which keeps the autocorrelation and destroys any alignment); a lead asymmetry A (mean r over lags 1 to 7 minus mean r over lags -1 to -7), with its permutation p-value; 7-lag Granger tests in both directions; and flagged-cluster timing (|z| > 2.5, each Malacca cluster paired to the nearest Singapore cluster within 14 days, against the same procedure on circularly shifted Singapore data). As in Part 2, everything is shown three ways: all out-of-sample days, excluding the first 90 days (the baseline edge window), and from 2022 (which also drops the COVID-era collapse). A causal high-pass series (log count minus its day-of-week mean minus its own trailing 28-day mean) is also run for total traffic, as a check that does not rely on extrapolating a trend.
  - *The baseline edge window matters more here than in Part 2.* In 2020-01-01 to 2020-03-30 the Malacca z-score averages -3.86 and the Singapore z-score +4.45 (container -1.36 and +3.55; tanker -3.82 and +3.78), opposite-signed artifacts of fitting on one year of data. Including them makes the zero-lag correlation negative for total traffic (-0.083) and tanker (-0.212); excluding them gives +0.263 and +0.088, and +0.277 and +0.083 from 2022 (container +0.170, +0.306, +0.332 across the three samples). Singapore's baseline is also weak out of sample (R^2 -0.283 for total port calls, -0.475 container, -0.135 tanker; Malacca +0.383, +0.425, -0.158), so these z-scores carry some baseline drift. Singapore has 83 flagged clusters overall and 79 once the first window is dropped (Malacca 129 and 122, as in Part 2).
  - *Result for total traffic.* Excluding the first 90 days, the zero-lag correlation is +0.263 against a 95% null band of -0.192 to +0.196: above chance but small, and the correlation stays between about 0.16 and 0.22 at every lag from -14 to +14, around the upper edge of the null band, which says both series are slow-moving and partly share drift, not that either leads. The lead asymmetry is -0.005 (permutation p = 0.58; -0.017, p = 0.09 from 2022): mean r is 0.187 over the Malacca-leads lags and 0.192 over the Singapore-leads lags. Container and tanker are the same: A is +0.004, -0.001, -0.001 for container and -0.004, -0.002, -0.009 for tanker across the three samples (p = 0.34 to 0.95), with a zero-lag peak for container (+0.306, null band -0.187 to +0.175) and no clear one for tanker (+0.088, band -0.117 to +0.096). The Granger tests are not informative here: for total and container traffic they are significant in both directions (most p < 0.001), which is what two persistent, partly co-moving series give, not evidence of order.
  - *Cluster timing says the same.* Excluding the first 90 days, 84 of Malacca's 122 clusters (69%) have a Singapore cluster within 14 days, against 56% (95th percentile 66%) when the Singapore series is shifted at random, so flagged days coincide a little more than chance. Among those pairs the median gap is 0 days, Malacca is first in 46% and Singapore first in 46%, and 24% fall within a day of each other. For container (71% against 65%, 95th percentile 75%) and tanker (55% against 46%, 95th percentile 59%) the share matched is not above chance.
  - *High-pass check.* A zero-lag correlation of +0.145 (null band about -0.04 to +0.04) from 2019-02, and +0.134 and +0.145 for the later samples, with essentially nothing at lags 1 to 7 in either direction (mean r 0.010 when Malacca leads and 0.032 when Singapore leads from 2019-02). The only hint of an order is a slight tendency for Singapore to move first (A = -0.023, p = 0.03 from 2019-02; -0.037, p = 0.004 from 2022; Singapore-to-Malacca Granger p < 0.001 in all three samples against 0.24 for Malacca-to-Singapore from 2022), but the correlations involved are about 0.03, it does not appear in the z-score series or in the cluster timing, and with six Malacca clusters from 2022 the cluster comparison has no power, so it is not a finding.
  - *Interpretation, and what this does not show.* A same-day relationship with no lead is what two-way traffic would give: ships going west leave Singapore and then transit Malacca, ships going east transit and then arrive, so neither series should consistently come first. That is reasoning, not tested, since the data has no direction of travel. The result does not rule out a lead on a finer time scale than daily counts, in specific vessel types or in specific events (six documented events are too few to test), or a relationship that only shows in the tails.
  - *A note on the clustering code.* `baseline.cluster_anomalies` documents that flagged days within 3 days form one cluster, but the code starts a new cluster whenever the previous day was not flagged, so in practice a cluster is a run of consecutive flagged days. The cluster counts quoted in this README (129, 122, 83) are what the code produces and are unchanged; only the description was off. `lead_lag.py` reproduces the code's behaviour exactly and asserts that it agrees on both real series. *Documentation-only fix, no effect on results: the `cluster_anomalies` docstring now describes what the code does; the logic is untouched, and every script's output and every file in `data/` regenerates byte-for-byte identical to the run before the edit.*
  - See `data/chart_lead_lag.png` and `data/lead_lag_xcorr.csv`, `lead_lag_summary.csv`, `lead_lag_clusters.csv`.
- **Broke the anomaly series down by vessel type (Part 4: `vessel_types.py`). Tankers carry most of the flagged days at both sites, the type matters little for which events show up, and only container (and the small roro series) co-moves across the two sites.**
  - *Data.* The saved files already carry the columns, so nothing new was fetched: Malacca transits by container, dry bulk, general cargo, roro and tanker, and Singapore port calls by the same five. Mean per day at Malacca: tanker 75.6 (38% of 199.3), dry bulk 51.4 (26%), container 50.7 (25%), general cargo 15.3 (8%), roro 6.3 (3%); at Singapore: tanker 71.9 (59% of 121.8), container 38.2 (31%), general cargo 5.7, roro 3.7, dry bulk 2.3 (zero on 341 days). Every type series goes through the same pipeline as the total (`baseline.py`, unchanged), fitted as log(count + 1) because several cannot be logged as they are. For counts this small the Gaussian z-score is a poor description of the noise. (`data/vessel_type_inventory.csv`.)
  - *Baseline quality.* Out-of-sample R^2 at Malacca: container 0.426, dry bulk 0.269, general cargo 0.147, tanker -0.157, roro -0.602 (total 0.383). At Singapore every type is below a flat average: container -0.476, dry bulk -0.092, general cargo -0.160, roro -0.463, tanker -0.133 (total -0.283). At Malacca, tanker (-0.157) and roro (-0.602) are below a flat average too. (`data/vessel_type_baseline.csv`.)
  - *Event matching by type* (clusters within 14 days of a documented event, excluding the first 90 days, against the match rate for randomly placed clusters of the same lengths). Malacca: container 61% (47 of 77; chance 56%), dry bulk 76% (47 of 62; 55%), general cargo 66% (39 of 59; 54%), roro 73% (40 of 55; 56%), tanker 68% (52 of 76; 57%), total 64% (78 of 122; 57%). Singapore: container 51% (59 of 116; 56%, below chance), dry bulk 58% (25 of 43; 56%), general cargo 64% (35 of 55; 56%), roro 57% (31 of 54; 56%), tanker 65% (35 of 54; 57%), total 72% (57 of 79; 56%). With 43 to 116 clusters a gap of 5 to 7 points is within sampling noise, so only Malacca dry bulk (+21 points), roro (+17) and the Singapore total (+17) stand clearly above chance, and no type separates the events better than the total does.
  - *Event by type.* The change in mean z from the 30 days before each event start to the 30 days from it, for each of 6 events and 10 type series, with a placebo p-value from 1,000 random dates in the same series (`data/vessel_type_event_changes.csv`). 7 of the 60 cells have p < 0.05, against about 3 expected by chance: Hormuz 2026 in Malacca tanker (-1.76, p = 0.024) and roro (-0.82, p = 0.032) and in Singapore dry bulk (-0.85, p = 0.014) and general cargo (-0.73, p = 0.033); Panama Canal restrictions in Malacca general cargo (-0.85, p = 0.041); the US ports strike in Singapore container (+0.83, p = 0.032); and COVID in Singapore dry bulk (+1.08, p = 0.002, inside the baseline edge window of Part 2). Hormuz is the only event where several types move, and the Malacca tanker drop (-1.76) is larger than the total's (-1.47), but Singapore's tanker calls do not move (-0.11). With 60 tests this is suggestive, not established.
  - *Which types move with the total* (excluding the first 90 days; the total has 184 flagged days at Malacca and 84 at Singapore). Malacca: tanker z correlates 0.79 with the total's, and 65% of the tanker's flagged days are also flagged in the total (31% of the total's flagged days are flagged in tanker); dry bulk 0.67 (48% and 19%), container 0.64 (35% and 15%), general cargo 0.54, roro 0.28. Singapore: tanker 0.83 (60% and 42%), container 0.55 (17% and 25%), the rest 0.26 or lower. Where a type and the total are both flagged, the sign agrees every time. (`data/vessel_type_vs_total.csv`.)
  - *The same type at both sites.* Same-day z correlation between Malacca and Singapore, excluding the first 90 days, with a 95% null band from 1,000 circular shifts: container +0.305 (band -0.181 to +0.191), roro +0.269 (-0.075 to +0.088), general cargo +0.097 (-0.095 to +0.069), total +0.263 (-0.196 to +0.195); dry bulk +0.057 and tanker +0.088 are inside their bands. The co-movement Part 3 found therefore comes from container and roro traffic and not from tankers, which dominate each site's total but are not correlated across sites. (`data/vessel_type_cross_site.csv`.)
  - Caveats: type-level baselines are worse than the total's, the series with small counts have little information (Singapore dry bulk, roro and general cargo have 2 to 6 calls a day), the event-by-type table is 60 tests on six events, and the data have no direction of travel. See `data/chart_vessel_types.png`.
- **Compared the baseline with two structurally different detectors (Part 5: `second_detector.py`). The flagged clusters overlap more than chance but are mostly not reproduced, and the match-to-events lift is not robust to the detector.**
  - *The detectors.* A is the existing one (trend + day of week + month, refit quarterly). B1 forecasts each day as the median of the same weekday over the previous 8 weeks, with z from 1.4826 x the median absolute deviation of the previous 56 days' errors. B2 is a seasonal decomposition: weekly seasonal indices from the previous 52 weeks, a level from the last 28 de-seasonalised days, z from the MAD of the previous 90 days' errors. Both are causal, have no trend or month term and no refit schedule, and use the same |z| > 2.5 rule, clustering and 14-day event matching. B2 needs 457 days of history, so all comparisons use the 2,347 days from 2020-04-02, which excludes the baseline edge window of Part 2. On these days A's counts are 184 flagged days, 122 clusters and 78 matched (64%), the Part 2 numbers without that window.
  - *Forecast accuracy.* On the same days the local baselines forecast better. Out-of-sample R^2 on the log scale and mean absolute error for the Malacca total: A 0.475 and 0.100, B1 0.729 and 0.071, B2 0.776 and 0.065; Singapore port calls: A 0.122 and 0.080, B1 0.248 and 0.074, B2 0.326 and 0.071. A's R^2 here is higher than the 0.383 in the model-selection table only because the edge window is left out. (`data/second_detector_summary.csv`.)
  - *Flagged days.* Malacca: A 184 days in 122 clusters, B1 58 in 45, B2 43 in 37. Singapore: A 84 in 79, B1 52 in 48, B2 35 in 35. A flags about three times as many days at Malacca, because a baseline with a global trend drifts away from the level for weeks at a time (panel A of the chart: runs below -2.5 in 2023 and again in 2026), where B1 and B2 re-centre within weeks and flag the onset of a change.
  - *Agreement.* z-scores correlate 0.66 (B1) and 0.68 (B2) with A at Malacca and 0.80 and 0.85 at Singapore. On daily flags, Cohen's kappa against A is 0.28 and 0.23 at Malacca and 0.32 and 0.36 at Singapore. At the cluster level, 31% (B1) and 26% (B2) of A's Malacca clusters have a B cluster within 3 days, against a median of 13% and 11% (95th percentile 19% and 16%) when B's flagged days are shifted circularly at random; at Singapore 35% and 32% against 14% and 9% (95th percentile 19% and 15%). In the other direction, 67% and 76% of B's Malacca clusters have an A cluster nearby (56% and 77% at Singapore), which is high because A flags so many days. With each detector limited to its 184 (Malacca) or 84 (Singapore) largest |z| days, A shares 37% (B1) and 39% (B2) of them at Malacca and 39% and 44% at Singapore. So there is a common core above chance, and roughly two thirds of A's flagged clusters are not reproduced by either alternative.
  - *Matching to documented events.* Share of clusters within 14 days of an event, against random placement of clusters of the same lengths: Malacca total A 64% (78 of 122; chance 56%), B1 60% (27 of 45; 57%), B2 62% (23 of 37; 55%); Singapore port calls A 72% (57 of 79; 56%), B1 56% (27 of 48; 55%), B2 51% (18 of 35; 56%). The Malacca lifts are similar in size (+8, +3, +7 points) but with 37 to 122 clusters only A's is comfortably beyond sampling noise; at Singapore the lift is specific to A and absent for both alternatives. The match-rate result is therefore not a property of the data alone, and the headline match rate should be read as weak support for a link to the events.
  - *The baseline edge window.* A's mean z over the first 90 out-of-sample days is -3.86 at Malacca (77 of 90 days flagged) and +4.45 at Singapore (87 of 90); B1's is -0.31 (6 flagged) and 0.00 (2 flagged), so the artifact is specific to the regression baseline fitted on a single year. B2 has no scores in that window.
  - *Event study with each detector's z* (Malacca total, 30 days from the start against the 30 days before, placebo p from 2,000 random dates): A -0.42 (p = 0.12) as in Part 2; B1 -0.09 (p = 0.61), and -0.05 without COVID (p = 0.80); B2 -0.07 without COVID (p = 0.64), with COVID not scoreable because B2's first score is after its start. The absolute-z change is +0.14 (p = 0.09) for B1 with all six events and +0.06 (p = 0.27) without COVID, and 0.00 (p = 0.51) for B2 without COVID. Events with a flagged day within 14 days: A 3 of 6, B1 2 of 6, B2 2 of 5. So the finding of Part 2, no distinguishable average path around event starts, holds for all three detectors. (`data/second_detector_event_study.csv`, `data/second_detector_clusters.csv`; see `data/chart_second_detector.png`.)

## Files
- `fetch_data.py` — pulls the raw daily data and disruption calendar from PortWatch's public API (no key required) into `data/`.
- `baseline.py` — the shared rolling out-of-sample fit, anomaly clustering and event matching, used by both scripts below.
- `model_selection.py` — the three-way target comparison above; regenerates every model-selection number in this README.
- `sensitivity_analysis.py` — checks whether the headline match-rate-vs-chance result holds across a grid of anomaly thresholds and matching windows; produces `data/sensitivity_grid.csv` and `data/chart_sensitivity_heatmap.png`.
- `event_study.py` — lines up the Malacca z-score on each documented event's start date and averages across events with a t-interval and a random-date placebo envelope, plus the baseline edge check; produces `data/chart_event_study.png` and the `data/event_study_*.csv` files behind the numbers above.
- `lead_lag.py` — Part 3: cross-correlation, lead asymmetry, Granger tests and flagged-cluster timing between the Malacca and Singapore anomaly series, by total/container/tanker, with and without the baseline edge window and COVID, plus a high-pass check; produces `data/chart_lead_lag.png` and the `data/lead_lag_*.csv` files behind the numbers above.
- `vessel_types.py`: Part 4: the baseline, clusters, event matching and an event-by-type table for each vessel type at both sites, the type-against-total overlap, and the same-type correlation across sites; produces `data/chart_vessel_types.png` and the `data/vessel_type_*.csv` files behind the numbers above.
- `second_detector.py`: Part 5: a weekly-median detector and a seasonal-decomposition detector run on the same series, compared with the baseline on accuracy, flagged days, cluster overlap against a shifted-flags null, event matching and the event study; produces `data/chart_second_detector.png` and `data/second_detector_*.csv`.
- `analysis.py` — builds the rolling out-of-sample baseline, flags anomalies, cross-references them against the disruption calendar, and produces `data/chart_overview.png`.
- `data/` — raw and processed CSVs, plus the anomaly cluster table.

## Possible extensions
- Bring in the Singapore Maritime Data Hub (MPA's own live vessel-movement API) to see whether Singapore-specific vessel positioning corroborates the port-level story at finer granularity.
- Extend the same rolling-baseline method to other Asia-Pacific chokepoints (Taiwan Strait, Korea Strait) PortWatch tracks, to see whether Malacca's anomaly timing is regional or Malacca-specific.
